The experiment

Is AnimateBanana's workflow self-discoverable by an agent having only our stage descriptions as skills and tools?

We use the closed-source models Opus 5 and Gemini 3.8 Flash (Gemini 3.7 is deprecated and no longer available) as the agent. The agent has AnimateBanana's stage descriptions as skills, and the raster-cropping and frame-rendering tools as callable tools, and it decides on its own how to use them. We wish to test how the agent makes use of these skills and tools, and whether, in doing so, it self-discovers our pipeline.

We test it under two conditions. In one, the agent is given a time budget based on the time our pipeline took to animate that diagram. In the other, there is no time limit. We observe the performance under both conditions.

Two closed-source baselines used as the main agent in a harness

Claude Code · Opus 5 Antigravity · Gemini 3.8 Flash

15 AnimateBench diagrams

A representative collection of diagrams with and without raster content.

A neutral, isolated workspace

The agent cannot see AnimateBanana's actual stages and has no clue about its representations. It only gets the stage descriptions, as skills and usable tools, and a fresh session per diagram with web access switched off.

The same prompt as the baselines

The prompt from the zero-shot diagram-to-animation task is reused, asking for an animated SVG and a timestamped narration that matches it. No clues are given on formatting, schemas or rubrics, and no examples.

A time budget per diagram

Each diagram's budget is the time AnimateBanana took to produce the animation for that same diagram, rounded up to the next minute.

Results · skills

Use of skills / stage hints across all runs

Without a time budget, the stage hints were used only partly. When the agents were given a time budget equal to the time AnimateBanana took on the same diagram, most of the stage hints were dropped, particularly by Claude, which treats them as optional and skips them.

Insights: how the two harnesses used the skills

GeminiGemini treats the skills as a checklist.

It reads every SKILL.md first and works through most of them.

ClaudeClaude treats them as optional, and bypasses them under budget.

For example, it often skips invoking the diagram-structure skill or the diagram-to-svg conversion, and probes the image with custom-generated Python scripts instead.

Clauderegion-crop became a magnifier.

It often uses the region-crop tool as a means of zooming in and reading labels in images, rather than using it to embed rasters in the output.

Results · time

Time taken by the agents, with and without a budget

Without a budget, the agents needed 1.4× (Claude) and 2.9× (Gemini) AnimateBanana's time. With AnimateBanana's time as a budget, both fit roughly inside it, but only by changing how they worked, as the next section shows.

Results · time

How a budget changes what the agents spend their time on

  • Understanding the image dominates for the agents. The autonomous agents spend most of their time understanding the image, using zoomed crops, grid overlays and NumPy scans, which take up 45–58% of their time. AnimateBanana perceives the image only once, in the converter, where it reconstructs the diagram from the image as SVG, and then parses the structure from its own SVG.
  • A budget cuts image understanding first. On introducing a budget, Claude cuts the share of its time spent understanding the image from 58% to 32%.
  • The agents check only the final animation. Most of the checks performed by the autonomous agents are on the final animation. They perform no checks on the SVG or on the sequence, as AnimateBanana does with its Stage-1 and Stage-2 critics.
Results · outputs

Qualitative examples of failure modes of the autonomous agent

Under a time budget, the agents prioritise producing the deliverable and sacrifice fidelity. We observe four kinds of patterns, and show examples of each. Click any example to open it large.

1

Photos replaced by simplified vector stand-ins

When given a time budget, Claude Opus 5 replaced every embedded raster/natural image in these four diagrams with simplified vector drawings, just as observed in the zero-shot diagram-to-animation task. In its own reasoning trace, it notes that a small time budget is unfavourable to a multi-skill pipeline, and it prefers to author the animation directly.

2

Revealing the source PNG instead of redrawing it

In several outputs, instead of redrawing the diagram elements, the agent built the animation by simply clipping regions from the source diagram image. This preserves visual fidelity but takes away from the editability of the results, and Claude states that it does this to save time.

4

Finishing early rather than improving

The agents prioritise finishing early over improving their work. In one example, on ICCV_2023_set5_000003, Claude wrote a single generator script and made one render check, with no review of its work, and stopped after using only 34% of its budget. Across runs, Claude used only 87% of its budget time on average and Gemini 95%, and neither spent the remaining time checking its work. This is why a dedicated, orchestrated pipeline with critics matters, rather than leaving the agent to its own devices.

Conclusion

What we conclude from this

  1. 1

    Recovering the order is not the same as reconstructing the workflow

    Without a time limit, both agents could work out much of the broad sequence: understand the diagram first, then decide how to present it. But following this order was not enough. The agent rarely turned the reconstructed diagram into a structured representation that could be reused by the later stages. This took them 1.4–2.9× longer than AnimateBanana.

  2. 2

    Under a time constraint, they took shortcuts

    Given the same time as AnimateBanana, both agents moved away from the stage-wise process and favoured direct authoring. Their AN scores were 0.577 (Claude) and 0.446 (Gemini), compared with 0.952 for AnimateBanana.

  3. 3

    Much of their effort went into understanding the image again

    Repeated image inspection took 32–58% of agent time. AnimateBanana reconstructs the diagram once and carries its structure forward through SVG and XML, so later stages can work with that representation rather than repeatedly recovering information from pixels. The agents, meanwhile, largely confined their checks to the final animation.

  4. 4

    The shortcuts came at a cost to fidelity

    Under time pressure, agents replaced embedded photographs with simplified drawings or animated the source image through cropping rather than reconstructing its elements. They finished faster, but with less faithful and less editable outputs.

Conclusion

This experiment suggests that stage descriptions are not enough to reproduce AnimateBanana. What matters is how the diagram is represented, how that representation is passed from one stage to the next, and how intermediate results are checked and refined. These choices are what make the workflow reliable within a practical time budget.
