Preparation

Task Explanation and Familiarization

Every participant worked through the same sequence: this preparation step, then the two main experiments. Trials were self-paced. An animation could be replayed, and stepped through as often as a participant liked before answering.

Before receiving study trials, participants complete a short guided preparation module explaining how to evaluate animated narratives, to help their understanding of the evaluation criteria.

The same module walks them through our custom portal: a guided tour of the controls they will use to view an animation, zoom the original figure, and answer the questions.

Protocol

Main User Study Experiments

Every participant did both experiments, in this order.

1

Animation quality assessment

The aim of this experiment is to evaluate the animation quality of the outputs produced by AnimateBanana, by the second-best open-source model (the one used as the backbone in our pipeline), and by the best closed-source model among those evaluated on the direct diagram-to-animation task.

Each of these baselines is judged in isolation: a participant rates one animation at a time and is never asked to compare. Closely following the cascading design of our automated metrics, we apply the same conditional principle here, opening with two gated questions that carry the core question behind each automated metric.

AnimateBanana (Gemini 3.7 Flash) Qwen3.8-27B backbone Gemini 3.1 Pro 15 trials per set one per animation style

Nothing is rated before it has been watched

A participant cannot answer anything until the animation has been played or stepped through to its very end. Until then the questions stay sealed behind a banner, the progress read-out shows how far through they are, and Submit and continue stays disabled. Judgements are therefore made on the whole animation, never on a glance at its opening frames.

Gate 1 · Visual fidelity

This is in close compliance with our Visual Fidelity metric, which asks how faithful the animation is to the source figure. And as with the accept and reject bands of the automated evaluation, the participant is given a binary accept or reject.

VF

Does the animation show all the elements of the diagram correctly?

Yes No

Only a yes moves the trial on to the compliance of the animation style. A no ends the sample there, and it scores 0.

Gate 2 · Animation style compliance

The expected animation style is stated at the top of the page, above the animation, and its exact rules open underneath it. This is assistive, so that a participant is gauging compliance against a written standard rather than an instinct about what the style might mean.

ASC

The expected animation style is X. Does the above animation follow this style?

Yes No

If style compliance is judged a no, evaluation of the sample ends there and then, and we do not go into the further stages. This is in close emulation of the gating mechanism followed in our automated evaluation, which is what keeps the two measures fair and comparable.

Both gates passed · Selection sensibility

With both gates cleared, we ask the participant to rate whether the choice of which element to animate next was a sensible one.

SS

Is the selection of the next element to animate a sensible one?

0 – 10 0: bad selection overall · 10: correct selection overall

This is in close compliance with our Selection Sensibility metric, which is computed at frame level, with a score for every frame aggregated into a final score. For ease on the participant's side we make them watch the animation step by step, and then give one overall rating.

Both gates passed · Narration alignment

The same thing is implemented for evaluating narration alignment. The participant reads the caption displayed at each step of the narration as it plays, and then gives a single overall score for how well it aligns with the animation.

NA

Does the narration align with the given animation?

0 – 10 0: misaligned and arbitrary · 10: well aligned and insightful

Once every question has an answer, Submit and continue becomes available and the participant moves on to the next sample.

Scores are normalised to [0, 1]; an animation that fails either gate scores 0. Granularity and pacing (GP) is not evaluated at all: the setup allows a self-paced traversal of the animation, with back and forth as often as the participant likes, so the pacing they experienced is their own rather than the system's. Each user-study set carries 15 animation quality assessment trials.

2

Ranking-based evaluation

The second experiment is run as a tournament. The candidates are the AnimateBanana outputs, the second-best performing model overall, in our case Qwen 3.8, and the original human-authored presentations from our bench.

Those human authored presentations are preprocessed and trimmed, in both time and space, to remove every clue that they are the human-authored ones. The whole experiment runs blind: a participant sees Left (A) against Right (B), never a system name.

AnimateBanana vs Qwen 3.8 → Winner vs Human-authored presentations → Rank 1

One question, and it is about personal utility

Ask

Which of these animations would you be most likely to use yourself?

Left (A) Right (B)

The question is strictly one of personal utility: if they had to pick one to use for their own presentation, which would they pick? The winner of this experiment is therefore the one participants prefer for their own use, a clearer win in terms of adoption by members of the scientific and research community than a simple quality assessment would give.

How a tournament plays out

Both animations must be watched or stepped to the end before the choice unlocks. The picked animation stays; the one passed over fades and is replaced by the third candidate for round two. The final pick takes rank 1, the one it beat rank 2, and the candidate eliminated first rank 3. A participant can return to an earlier trial and change an answer before it is saved.

Protocol

Selective Cohort Experiments

Ten participants returned for two further preference tests, each over 15 samples. Both ask about a design decision rather than about a system.

1

Context utility

The first experiment of the selective cohort assesses the usefulness of context in producing the narration captions: are captions derived with context more useful than narration derived from the figure alone?

The same AnimateBanana animation is shown twice, differing only in its narration. One is drafted with the caption and paper context, the other from the figure by itself. Neither side is labelled.

Ask

Which narration is more insightful in understanding the animation?

First Second Both are equally useful

We deliberately allow for the possibility that both are equally useful in understanding the given narration, an outcome that is entirely plausible given the depth of the context provided.

2

AnimateBench verification

The last experiment in the selective cohort is bench verification, a quality-assurance pass of our own: it validates whether the corrections made in the bench are meaningful, by asking whether they introduce an improvement in the perceived quality of the animations.

Ask

Do the corrections result in a noticeable improvement in the perceived quality of the animation?

Yes No

This one is not run blind. The uncorrected and the corrected animation are clearly demarcated, labelled Original (uncorrected) and Verified and corrected, and the participant is told as much before the section begins.
